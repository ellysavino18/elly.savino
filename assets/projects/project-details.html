Trophic Structure of the Pelagic Community

A multi-tissue approach using Stable Isotope Analysis (SIA) and Stomach Content Analysis (SCA) to understand trophic dynamics in the Western Mediterranean Sea.

  1. Home
  2. Project Details

Context & Objectives

Context: The Mediterranean Sea is under intense stress from anthropogenic activities and climate change. Understanding the intricate food web and trophic dynamics of pelagic fish communities is a fundamental step for effective marine conservation and ecosystem-based management.

Objectives: The primary objective was to reconstruct the trophic dynamics of pelagic fish communities in the western Mediterranean Sea, using a novel multi-tissue approach.To achieve this, we set two complementary objectives:

  • 1. Methodological Comparison: We compared the trophic information derived from two different methodologies: Stomach Content Analysis (SCA) and Stable Isotope Analysis (SIA). The aim was to understand whether these two methods showed similar or divergent information regarding trophic relationships.
  • 2. Tissue Variability Analysis: We analyzed the isotopic variability across different tissues, distinguishing between metabolically active tissues (subject to physiological turnover) and inert tissues (which act as a chronological archive of the individual's life).

Methodology

Sample Processing

Dissection and preparation of multiple tissues (muscle, bone, eyes, gonads, liver, vertebra, and dorsal fin) to capture different ecological timeframes.

Analytical Techniques

Integration of Stable Isotope Analysis (SIA) with traditional Stomach Content Analysis (SCA) to cross-validate dietary habits.

Data Modeling

Statistical analysis, isotopic niche modeling, and data visualization performed entirely using R and spatial mapping in QGIS.

Key Takeaways

  • The "Invisible" Diets: Comparing the two methodologies clarified that while Stomach Content Analysis (SCA) often overestimates trophic levels due to digestive bias, Stable Isotope Analysis (SIA) reveals that the growth of juvenile large pelagics is actually sustained by lower trophic resources (like zooplankton) which often remain invisible to the stomach.
  • Reading the "Isotopic Clock": The multi-tissue approach demonstrated that the isotopic signal changes from one tissue to another. This variability reflects the different assimilation and turnover rates, linked to the specific physiology of each tissue and species. This confirms that the trophic structure is not static: different tissues allow us to reconstruct different time windows, from recent activity to the organism's entire life history.
  • The Need for Complementarity: Ultimately, this study highlights that relying on a single method or tissue can lead to a partial view. The results confirm that combining traditional stomach analysis with multi-tissue isotopic analysis is strictly necessary to better understand the complexity of pelagic ecosystems.
